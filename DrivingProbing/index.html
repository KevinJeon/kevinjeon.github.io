Does good performance in closed-loop simulation guarantee a robust driving policy?

The motivation

Beyond closed-loop scores

Real driving logs contain many simple straight-driving scenes, and a model can look strong from the ego’s own state alone.12 Closed-loop simulation is supposed to be the stricter test. BC and RL do improve there.

These scores do not reveal whether the policy learned generalizable prediction and planning or a heuristic that works in ordinary scenes.

What a robust policy should do

A robust policy should respond when the scene changes, distinguish relevant from irrelevant vehicles, and maintain plans for safety-critical interactions.

Respond to changes

A slowing lead car should slow the ego. A lead car that leaves should let the ego go.

Identify relevant vehicles

A nearby car that can enter the path matters. A distant car with no bearing on the plan does not.

Plan for critical interactions

The policy should stay ready for an abrupt cut-in, not only for the ordinary scenes it has already seen.

Our approach

Look inside the policy, and perturb the simulation.

We combine linear probing with perturbed simulation and targeted interventions to examine prediction and planning in behavior cloning and reinforcement learning policies.

Prediction

Where surrounding vehicles will move next.

Planning

How the ego’s future plan adapts to changes in those predictions.

Linear probes measure information accessible through a simple linear readout of the policy’s representation. We investigate three connected questions.

01 Linear probing with scale

Surrounding-vehicle prediction remains imperfect.

For BC, probing gains rise through about 10K scenes and then plateau. RL does not follow that curve: from 5K to 10K, driving and ego-plan probing improve while surrounding-vehicle probing weakens.

The gains are uneven across trajectory types. BC improves more for Straight and Uncategorized futures than for Turn and Reverse cases. Relative probing gain, LP − Raw, measures the additional linearly accessible information beyond a probe trained directly on raw input.

Which nearby vehicles are represented more strongly?

At 100 scenes, the relationship with future distance is weak. At 10K and 80K, the association is more negative: the probing advantage is stronger for vehicles that stay close. Among nearby vehicles, however, approaching and receding cars have similar probing signals. Scaling does not clearly distinguish which nearby vehicle will become most interaction-relevant.

02 Distance-ordered vehicle removal

Limited generalization to single-AV settings.

We progressively remove surrounding vehicles in nearest-first and farthest-first order. The two strategies reveal an asymmetry in the policy’s sensitivity to surrounding-vehicle context.

Removing nearby vehicles increasingly degrades goal progress at high removal ratios, even as adjusted vehicle-collision rate decreases.

Removing distant vehicles has little effect on goal progress through high removal ratios, indicating relative insensitivity to distant-agent context.

Adjusted vehicle-collision rate = Veh-Coll / (1 − p), where p is the fraction of surrounding vehicles removed. This rate is normalized by the remaining-vehicle ratio and is undefined at p = 1.

These results suggest brittleness to the removal of nearby-agent context rather than increased interaction difficulty. Naturally occurring single-AV scenes already perform worse than normal scenes; stripping vehicles from an originally interactive scene causes a further decline.

Together, the results suggest limited generalization to reduced-interaction settings and sensitivity to nearby-agent context, rather than a general spurious reliance on surrounding vehicles.

03 Prediction and planning

When prediction signals emerge—and what changes when we edit them.

Near-collision analysis

An early warning would appear as a strong positive probing signal 40, 30, or 20 steps before impact. For both BC and RL, the strongest signal appears mainly in the final 10 steps, concentrated near the ego.

RL collisions are rarer, leaving fewer samples and more filtered regions, but the same late peak remains. In these collision episodes, the relevant vehicle becomes strongly linearly decodable only shortly before impact.

Intervention: adaptiveness and recovery

We edit the surrounding-vehicle representation along a linear-probe direction and propagate that edit through the policy. Two measures test how the intervention affects ego behavior. Both settings are compared with magnitude-matched random controls.

Adaptiveness

CAP

CAP = mean(dafter − dbefore)
Schematic of one timestep; CAP averages over the horizon.

We induce a conflicting future for another vehicle. Positive CAP means the ego trajectory moves farther from that induced trajectory than before the intervention.

Recovery

GPG

GPG = gpint − gpbase
Schematic only; goal progress is measured in simulation.

We correct an erroneous surrounding-vehicle prediction toward its logged future. Positive GPG means that goal progress improves relative to the unmodified policy.

Evaluation subsets and interpretation

Low-progress recovery rows follow Appendix G, Table 14(c,d): baseline goal progress is at most 0.5, and only scenes whose outcomes change are included. The BC and RL subsets are not the same as their full recovery evaluation sets. BC and RL GPG is measured in closed-loop simulation; the accompanying example figures show linear-probe readouts.

For BC, the semantic edit moves the ego trajectory away from the induced conflict and improves goal progress, including in low-progress scenes. For RL, the representation follows the edit, but that change does not consistently propagate to ego planning.

Recovery does not imply uniform safety gains. In BC, collision and off-road rates increase slightly despite better goal progress.
