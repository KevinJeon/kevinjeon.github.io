Does a strong closed-loop score mean
the policy learned a generalized behavior?

Previous papers12 show that real driving logs are full of simple straight-driving scenes, and that a model can look strong from the ego’s own state alone. Closed-loop simulation is supposed to be the stricter test. BC and RL do improve there.

Closed-loop metrics against dataset size. BC largely saturates beyond about 20K scenes. RL reaches lower collision and off-road rates, and about 99% goal progress at 10K.

Did the policy learn a generalized driving policy it can carry into a new situation, or a heuristic that works because the scene is ordinary?

Robust driving policy

Closed-loop performances do not say which of these the policy actually has. A robust policy should respond when the scene changes, tell a relevant car from an irrelevant one, and keep a plan for the moment that becomes dangerous.

Respond to a change

A slowing lead car should slow the ego. A lead car that leaves should let the ego go.

Tell cars apart

A nearby car that can enter the path matters. A distant car with no bearing on the plan does not.

Keep a plan for the critical case

The policy should stay ready for an abrupt cut-in, not only for the ordinary scene it has already seen.

Beyond the existed closed-loop evaluation, use perturbed simulation and linear probe.

Prediction. Where the surrounding vehicles will move next.

Planning. How the ego’s future plan changes

A linear probe reads whether prediction and planning are already in the policy’s representation, for both behavior cloning and reinforcement learning. We find three things.

  1. Linear Probe with scaling. Surrounding-vehicle prediction is insufficient.
  2. Perturbed Simulation with removal by distance. Not generzlied in single-AV driving, and performance changes.
  3. Linking the internal prediction and planning to the decision. Recover the prediction error and the plan change.

1 Linear Probe with scaling

Surrounding-vehicle prediction is insufficient.

LP − Raw, so BC and RL can be compared even though they do not see the same input. Higher is a stronger linear readout.

The overall probe score climbs with scale. The climb is uneven. Straight and uncategorized futures account for most of the gain, while turns and reverses stay difficult. For BC the gain rises through about 10K scenes and then plateaus. RL does not follow that curve: from 5K to 10K, driving and the ego-plan probe improve while the surrounding-vehicle probe gets weaker.

Probe score against the other car’s future distance from the ego. A negative slope means closer cars are read more strongly.

The probe does learn to care less about far cars. At 100 scenes the link to future distance is weak. At 10K and 80K it is clearly negative. Among the cars that stay close, though, one that is approaching and one that is moving away look alike. More data has not yet said which nearby car will matter.

2 Perturbed Simulation with removal by distance.

Not generzlied in single-AV driving, and performance changes.

Nearest-first removal. Adjusted collision is Veh-Coll / (1 − p), so having fewer cars to hit is not counted as safer driving.

Farthest-first removal. The adjusted vehicle-collision rate is the left-hand panel. Goal progress barely moves as distant cars disappear.

The two removal strategies produce a clear asymmetry. Farthest-first removal has little effect on goal progress through high removal ratios, indicating relative insensitivity to distant-agent context. In contrast, nearest-first removal increasingly degrades goal progress at high removal ratios, even as the adjusted vehicle-collision rate decreases. This suggests that the degradation reflects brittleness to the removal of nearby-agent context rather than increased interaction difficulty. Interestingly, the adjusted vehicle collision rate increases sharply under farthest-first removal, even though the farthest agent has little more impact on collisions than a nearby one.

Scenes that naturally contain only the ego are already worse than ordinary scenes. Stripping cars out of a scene that began as interactive makes performance worse still. The policy generalizes poorly to the single-vehicle case.

3 Linking the internal prediction and planning to the decision.

Recover the prediction error and the plan change.

Each cell is the probe in the pre-collision window, compared with the average over the whole episode. Cells with fewer than 100 collision cases are gray.

An early warning would light up 40, 30, or 20 steps before impact. For both BC and RL the signal stays weak until the last 10 steps, when it concentrates on the cars near the ego. The relevant car becomes readable only once the response is already late. RL collisions are rarer, and the same late peak remains.

We then edit the surrounding-vehicle representation along the probe and decode the ego plan again. Two measures say whether that edit reaches the decision.

CAP, adaptiveness

We induce a conflicting future for the other car. Positive CAP means the decoded ego plan moved farther from that future than a random edit of the same size.

GPG, recovery

We correct the surrounding-vehicle prediction. GPG is goal progress after the edit minus goal progress before it. Positive means the ego finished closer to its goal.

For BC, the real edit moves the plan away from the induced conflict and lifts goal progress, including on the scenes that were already failing. Safety does not follow that recovery. For RL, the representation follows the edit and the plan mostly does not. The internal state changes. The decision often does not.
